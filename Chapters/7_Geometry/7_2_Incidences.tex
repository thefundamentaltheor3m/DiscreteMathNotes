\section{Point--Line Incidences}

The crossing lemma also bounds how often points of the plane can lie on lines, and the bound in turn says something about sums and products of integers.

\subsection{The Szemer\'{e}di--Trotter Theorem}

Given finite sets $\mathcal{L}$ of lines and $P$ of points in $\R^2$, define
\begin{align*}
    I\of{\mathcal{L}, P}
    := \#\!\set{\text{point-line incidences}}
    = \#\!\setst{\parenth{\ell, p} \in \mathcal{L} \times P}{p \in \ell}
\end{align*}

It turns out that we can say how large this can be, up to a constant.

\begin{boxtheorem}[Szemer\'{e}di--Trotter] \label{Ch7:Thm:Szemeredi_Trotter}
    $I\of{\mathcal{L}, P} = \BigO{\abs{\mathcal{L}}^{\frac{2}{3}} \abs{P}^{\frac{2}{3}} + \abs{\mathcal{L}} + \abs{P}}$
\end{boxtheorem}
\begin{proof}
    Given an $\mathcal{L},P$ system, consider the graph drawing $D$ whose vertices are the points in $P$ and whose edges are intervals of lines in $\mathcal{L}$ with endpoints in $P$, with no interior points in $P$.

\subsection{Sums and Products}

Fix some $A \ssq \Z$. Define
\begin{align*}
    A + A &:= \setst{a + b}{a, b \in A} \\
    A \cdot A &:= \setst{a \cdot b}{a, b \in A}
\end{align*}

Is there a set $A$ for which both $A + A$ and $A \cdot A$ are small?

To answer this, we turn it into an incidence problem. Define
\begin{align*}
    P := \parenth{A + A} \times \parenth{A \cdot A}
    = \setst{(x, y) \in \Z \times \Z}{x \in A + A, \, y \in A \cdot A}
\end{align*}
For example, when $A = \set{1, 2, 3}$, we have $A + A = \set{2, \ldots, 6}$ and $A \cdot A = \set{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9}$, and $P$ looks like this.

        \draw[->] (1.5, -0.3) -- (1.5, 10) node[above] {$y$};
        \foreach \x in {2, ..., 6}{
            \draw (\x, 0.15) -- (\x, -0.15) node[below] {\small $\x$};

        \foreach \y in {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9}{
            \draw (1.6, \y) -- (1.4, \y) node[left] {\small $\y$};
            \foreach \x in {2, ..., 6}{
                \fill[black] (\x, \y) circle (2pt);
            }

        \node at (4, -1.6) {$A + A$};

\end{figure}

The incidence bound answers our question: $A + A$ and $A \cdot A$ cannot both be small.

% [FILLED] statement and outline written up from the blackboard photographs supplied with the post-lecture pass of 2026-10-27; the two \sorry steps are left as exercises, with hints
\begin{boxtheorem} \label{Ch7:Thm:Sum_Product}
    There is a constant $c > 0$ such that for every finite $A \ssq \Z$,
    \begin{align*}
        \abs{A + A} \abs{A \cdot A} \geq c \abs{A}^{\frac{5}{2}}
    \end{align*}
    In particular, $\max\set{\abs{A + A}, \abs{A \cdot A}} \geq \sqrt{c} \abs{A}^{\frac{5}{4}}$.
\end{boxtheorem}
\begin{proof}
    We may assume that $0 \notin A$: removing $0$ from $A$ can only shrink $A + A$ and $A \cdot A$, and for $\abs{A} \geq 2$ it at most halves $\abs{A}$, which changes only the constant.

    Our points are those of $P$, so $\abs{P} = \abs{A + A} \abs{A \cdot A}$. For the lines, take
    \begin{align*}
        \mathcal{L} := \setst{\text{the line } y = a\parenth{x - a'}}{a, a' \in A}
    \end{align*}
    The line $y = a\parenth{x - a'}$ has slope $a$ and, since $a \neq 0$, meets the $x$-axis exactly at $a'$, so different pairs $\parenth{a, a'}$ give different lines and $\abs{\mathcal{L}} = \abs{A}^2$. Each of these lines passes through at least $\abs{A}$ points of $P$, so
    \begin{align*}
        I\of{\mathcal{L}, P} \geq \abs{\mathcal{L}} \abs{A} = \abs{A}^3
    \end{align*}
    \sorry
    % Hint: fix the line $y = a\parenth{x - a'}$. Which $x$ make $y$ land in $A \cdot A$? Try $x = a' + b$ for each $b \in A$, and check that the point you get lies in $P$.

    On the other hand, \Cref{Ch7:Thm:Szemeredi_Trotter} gives a constant $C$ such that
    \begin{align*}
        \abs{A}^3
        \leq I\of{\mathcal{L}, P}
        \leq C\parenth{\abs{A}^{\frac{4}{3}} \abs{P}^{\frac{2}{3}} + \abs{A}^2 + \abs{P}}
    \end{align*}
    One of the three terms in the bracket is at least $\frac{\abs{A}^3}{3C}$, and in each case we get $\abs{P} \geq c\abs{A}^{\frac{5}{2}}$ for a suitable $c > 0$ depending only on $C$. \sorry
    % Hint: take the three cases one at a time. The first is where the exponent $\frac{5}{2}$ comes from. In the second, $\abs{A}$ is at most a constant, and $\abs{A + A}, \abs{A \cdot A} \geq \abs{A}$ (why?). The third is immediate.

    The second inequality follows from the first, since the larger of two numbers is at least the square root of their product.
\end{proof}
