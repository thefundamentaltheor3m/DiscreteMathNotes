\begin{boxdefinition}[Intersecting]
    We say that a $k$-uniform hypergraph $H$ is \textbf{intersecting} if $\forall h_1, h_2 \in H$, $h_1 \cap h_2 \ne \emptyset$.
\end{boxdefinition}

The question is: how big can an intersecting $k$-uniform hypergraph be? Ie, how many edges can it have? % [CLAUDE] polish the setup of the content a little better

\begin{itemize}
    \item $k = 1$: we can see that the maximum number of edges must be $1$.
    \item $k = 2$: the maximum number of edges is $n - 1$, achieved by the `star graph' % [CLAUDE] draw a picture of a point in the middle and edges radiating out from it
    \item $k = 3$: $\binom{n-1}{2}$, achieved by fixing one vertex that lies in everything and choosing $2$ more.
    \item $k \leq \frac{n}{2}$: $\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ by exactly the same strategy.
    \item $k > \frac{n}{2}$: we can do better! $\binom{n}{k}$ works because of something involving pigeons and holes.
\end{itemize}

\begin{boxtheorem}[\Erdos-Ko-Rado]
    If $n \geq 2k$, and $H$ is an intersecting $k$-uniform hypergraph on $n$ vertices, then the number of hyper-edges of $H$ is $\leq \binom{n-1}{k-1}$. Moreover, if $n \geq 2k + 1$, then the (``unique'') extremal example is given by all $k$-subsets containing a fixed vertex.
\end{boxtheorem}

As a warmup case, let's assume that $k \mid n$. Suppose $H$ is intersecting. Let $F$ denote some partition of $H$ into subsets of size $k$ (so $F$ is fundamentally a collection of subsets).
\begin{align*}
    X = \abs{\setst{h \in H}{h \in \pi(f) \text{ for some } f \in F}}
\end{align*}
where $\pi \in S_n$ is a permutation chosen uniformly at random. % [CLAUDE] I can't read what's written, double-check that what I've written here is accurate (flag when passing to /check-correctness)

Note that $X \leq 1$. Let's now compute its expected value.
\begin{align*}
    \E(X) &= \sum_{h \in H} \sum_{f \in F} \Pr\of{h = \pi(f)} \\
    &= \abs{H} \cdot \frac{n}{k} \cdot \frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}
\end{align*}
So since $X \leq 1$, we conclude that
\begin{align*}
    \abs{H} \cdot \frac{n}{k} \cdot \frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}} \leq \E(X) \leq 1
\end{align*}
Rearranging gives us that
\begin{align*}
    \abs{H} \leq \binom{n}{k} \cdot \frac{k}{n} = \binom{n-1}{k-1}
\end{align*}

Now let's prove the result in full generality.

\begin{align*}

A given $h$ is an interval of the randomly ordered cycle exactly when $\pi$ lays its $k$ elements out consecutively, and we can count the $\pi$ that do so. \begin{align*}

However we lay the $n$ vertices out around a cycle, then, exactly $k$ of that cycle's $k$-intervals are edges of $H$, and $k$ pairwise intersecting $k$-intervals are the $k$ intervals through a single vertex. Write $x_{\pi}$ for that vertex.

    It remains to see that $x_{\pi}$ does not depend on $\pi$, and one transposition at a time is enough. Fix an ordering, put $x = x_{\pi}$ at position $0$, number the other positions $1, \ldots, n - 1$ round the circle, and write $I_s$ for the interval occupying positions $s, s + 1, \ldots, s + k - 1$, so that the edges of $H$ that are intervals are exactly $I_{-(k-1)}, \ldots, I_0$. Now transpose the vertices at two adjacent positions $q$ and $q + 1$, neither of them position $0$. The only intervals this disturbs are the two holding exactly one of those positions, namely $I_{q+1}$ and $I_{q-k+1}$. But $I_{q+1}$ is one of ours only for $q \in \set{-k, \ldots, -1}$ and $I_{q-k+1}$ only for $q \in \set{0, \ldots, k-1}$, and once $q = 0$ and $q = -1$ are excluded those two ranges are disjoint, because $n \geq 2k + 1$. So at most one of our $k$ edges stops being an interval and at least $k - 1$ of them survive.

    The new ordering has exactly $k$ of its own intervals in $H$, again a run of $k$ consecutive ones. If nothing was lost, that run is ours. If what was lost came from the middle of ours, the survivors still include $I_{-(k-1)}$ and $I_0$, and no run of $k$ consecutive intervals but ours contains both. That leaves only the possibility that the interval lost was at one end, say $I_0$; the survivors are then $I_{-(k-1)}, \ldots, I_{-1}$, and the run of $k$ containing them is either ours or the one obtained by adding $I_{-k}$. But $I_{-k}$ occupies positions $-k, \ldots, -1$, so the transposition left it alone and it misses position $0$---making it an interval of the old ordering that was not an edge of $H$. So the run is ours after all, and the other end is the same argument with $I_1$ in place of $I_{-k}$. Either way the new ordering's distinguished vertex is again $x$.

    Adjacent transpositions avoiding position $0$ generate every arrangement of the other $n - 1$ vertices around the circle, so $x_{\pi} = x$ for every $\pi$. Take any $k$-subset $h$ of $[n]$ containing $x$, and lay the vertices out with the elements of $h$ consecutive: then $h$ is an interval containing $x$, so $h \in H$. There are $\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ such $h$, which is exactly $\abs{H}$, so they are all of it.
\end{proof}
