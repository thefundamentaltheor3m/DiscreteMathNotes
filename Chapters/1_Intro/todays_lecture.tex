\subsection{Conditional Expectation}

% [CORRECTED] was: "Given an event $A$ and a random variable $X$" --- at $\Pr(A) = 0$ the display below is $\frac{0}{0}$
% (every singleton in a null event is null, so the numerator vanishes too), so it defines nothing there, and the
% second display needs the same hypothesis, $\Pr\of{X = r \mid A}$ dividing by $\Pr(A)$ in its turn.
Given an event $A$ with $\Pr(A) \neq 0$ and a random variable $X$, we can define
\begin{align*}
    \E\of{X \mid A} = \frac{1}{\Pr(A)} \sum_{x \in A} X(x) \cdot p(x)
\end{align*}
where $p(x)$ is the density function. It is easy to show that
\begin{align*}
    \E\of{X \mid A} = \sum_{r \in X[\Omega]} r \cdot \Pr\of{X = r \mid A}
\end{align*}
% [CORRECTED] was: "$y_0 \mapsto \E\of{X \mid Y = y_0}$" in the display below --- that map has domain $\R$, not $\Omega$,
% so it is not a random variable in the sense of 3.1 and the outer $\E$ of the tower property has nothing to
% integrate against $\Pr$. Composing with $Y$ is what the next sentence claims has been done.
Given \textit{two} random variables $X$ and $Y$, we denote by $\E\of{X \mid Y}$ the \textbf{random variable}
\begin{align*}
    \omega \mapsto \E\of{X \mid Y = Y(\omega)}
\end{align*}

\begin{boxtheorem}[Tower Property of Conditional Expectation]
    For all random variables $X$ and $Y$,
    \begin{align*}
        \E\of{\E\of{X \mid Y}} = \E(X)
    \end{align*}
\end{boxtheorem}

The above type-checks because $\E\of{X \mid Y}$ is a random variable.

% Not from the lecture: the lecture defined the notation for one conditioning variable and then used it for several; the extension below was supplied.
Several random variables at once are no different: $\E\of{X \mid Y_1, \, \ldots, \, Y_k}$ is the random variable $\omega \mapsto \E\of{X \mid Y_1 = Y_1(\omega), \, \ldots, \, Y_k = Y_k(\omega)}$, conditioning on the one event that records what all of them did.

Let's study the expected value of chromatic numbers. Consider the family of random graphs $G_{3, \frac{1}{2}}$ of graphs on $3$ vertices with the probability of an edge between them being $\frac{1}{2}$. This makes $\chi\of{G_{3, \frac{1}{2}}}$ a random variable. What would we expect its expectation to be?

Fix an order $e_1, e_2, e_3$ on the three possible edges and look at them one at a time. The root of the tree stands for the whole family, before anything has been looked at; its left child for the graphs in which $e_1$ is present and its right child---the dashed branch---for those in which it is absent. Those two split the same way on $e_2$, and their children on $e_3$, so that the eight leaves are the eight graphs on three vertices and every node is the family of graphs agreeing with the exposures made along the way down to it. At each node we have written the expected chromatic number of a graph drawn from the family it stands for, which for a leaf is just the chromatic number of the one graph there: $1$ for the empty graph, $2$ for anything with one or two edges, and $3$ for the triangle. Every node is the average of the two beneath it, so the $2$ at the root really is $\E\of{\chi\of{G_{3, \frac{1}{2}}}}$, and reading the tree downwards is watching that expectation be refined one edge at a time.

Write $G$ for $G_{3, \frac{1}{2}}$.

We can adapt this to the general $G_{n, \frac{1}{2}}$ case. Define $Y_i$ and $X_i$ as above. Then, we can compute the conditional expectation of $\chi\of{G_{n, \frac{1}{2}}}$ on observing edges incident on vertices $1, \ldots, k$: take $Y_k$ to record which of the edges from vertex $k$ to vertices $1, \ldots, k-1$ are present and, now writing $X$ for $\chi\of{G_{n, \frac{1}{2}}}$, take $X_k = \E\of{X \mid Y_1, \ldots, Y_k}$, so that $X_n = X$ and, vertex $1$ having nothing behind it to expose, $X_1 = \E(X)$; put $X_0 = \E(X)$ as well, and $X_k = X$ for $k > n$, so that the sequence is one the definition covers. This exposes the graph a vertex at a time rather than an edge at a time, and it is what makes the first condition hold: two graphs differing only in the edges at a single vertex have chromatic numbers differing by at most $1$, since a proper coloring of either becomes a proper coloring of the other once that vertex is given a color of its own. That survives the averaging: $X_k$ is the average of the possible values of $X_{k+1}$, and any two of those differ by at most $1$, because pairing each unexposed configuration under one value of $Y_{k+1}$ with the very same configuration under another matches up two graphs differing only at vertex $k+1$. So $\abs{X_{k+1} - X_k} \leq 1$.

The point of insisting on that condition is the following, which says that a martingale cannot get far from where it started---and by the same margin \Cref{Ch3:Thm:Chernoff} gives a $\pm 1$ walk, which is the martingale all the others are measured against.

\begin{boxtheorem}[Azuma's Inequality] \label{Ch3:Thm:Azuma}
    Let $X_0, X_1, \ldots$ be a martingale and let $m$ be a positive integer. Then for every $\lambda > 0$,
    \begin{align*}
        \Pr\of{X_m - X_0 \geq \lambda \sqrt{m}} \leq e^{-\frac{\lambda^2}{2}}
    \end{align*}
\end{boxtheorem}

We won't prove it: the argument is the one behind \Cref{Ch3:Thm:Chernoff}, with the chord bound of that section applied to each increment $X_{k+1} - X_k$ conditionally on what has been exposed, which is legitimate precisely because the increments are bounded by $1$ and have conditional mean $0$.

What it says about the chromatic number is that $\chi\of{G_{n, \frac{1}{2}}}$ sits in a window of width about $\sqrt{n}$ around its mean---rather narrower than $\chi$ itself, which grows like $\frac{n}{\log n}$, a fact from outside the course that we take on trust here.

\begin{boxcorollary} \label{Ch3:Cor:Chromatic_Concentration}
    For every $n \geq 1$ and every $\lambda > 0$,
    \begin{align*}
        \Pr\of{\abs{\chi\of{G_{n, \frac{1}{2}}} - \E\of{\chi\of{G_{n, \frac{1}{2}}}}} \geq \lambda \sqrt{n}} \leq 2 e^{-\frac{\lambda^2}{2}}
    \end{align*}
\end{boxcorollary}
\begin{proof}
    The martingale above has $X_0 = \E\of{\chi\of{G_{n, \frac{1}{2}}}}$ and $X_n = \chi\of{G_{n, \frac{1}{2}}}$, so \Cref{Ch3:Thm:Azuma} at $m = n$ bounds $\Pr\of{X_n - X_0 \geq \lambda \sqrt{n}}$ by $e^{-\frac{\lambda^2}{2}}$. Applying it to $-X_0, -X_1, \ldots$, a martingale for the same reasons, bounds the other tail.
\end{proof}
