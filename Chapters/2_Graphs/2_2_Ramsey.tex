\section{Ramsey Numbers}

Everything so far has asked when a colouring exists. The opposite question is at least as natural: how large does a structure have to be before every colouring of it is forced to contain something monochromatic? Let's step back into ``graph land'' from ``hypergraph land'' and ask it of the complete graphs, colouring edges this time rather than vertices.

\begin{boxdefinition}[Ramsey Number]
    The $k$th Ramsey number, denoted $R(k)$, is the smallest $n \in \N$ such that any $2$-colouring of edges of $K_n$ gives a monochromatic $K_k$.
\end{boxdefinition}

One immediate result we can see is that $R(3) \geq 6$, which we can see simply by drawing a picture of $K_5$ and studying what happens if we move our attention to $K_6$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale = 2]
        \begin{scope}[red]
            \stepedges{5}{1}
        \end{scope}
        \begin{scope}[blue]
            \stepedges{5}{2}
        \end{scope}
        \cyclevertices{5}
    \end{tikzpicture}
\end{figure}

Each colour carries a $5$-cycle---the outer pentagon in red, the inner pentagram in blue---and a $5$-cycle contains no triangle at all, so neither colour gives us a monochromatic $K_3$. Nothing forces a monochromatic triangle at $n = 5$, and so $R(3) \geq 6$. The picture also shows how little room there is to spare: every vertex of $K_5$ meets exactly two edges of each colour, and the moment we pass to $K_6$ each vertex has five edges to distribute between two colours, so one of the colours is forced to take three of them.

We can actually prove that $R(3) = 6$, but this is rather nontrivial.

Other known Ramsey numbers are $R(2) = 2$ and $R(4) = 18$. The rest are unknown, though we do have bounds for them. The best (or close to the best) known bounds are
\begin{align*}
    2^{\frac{k}{2}} \leq R(k) \leq 3.8^k
\end{align*}

Upper bounds of this kind come from a recursion, which is cleanest stated for the off-diagonal Ramsey number $R(k, \ell)$: the smallest $n \in \N$ such that any red/blue colouring of the edges of $K_n$ gives a red $K_k$ or a blue $K_\ell$, so that $R(k) = R(k, k)$.

\begin{boxtheorem} \label{Ch2:Thm:Ramsey_Recursion}
    For all $k, \ell \geq 2$, we have
    \begin{align*}
        R(k, \ell) \leq R(k - 1, \ell) + R(k, \ell - 1)
    \end{align*}
\end{boxtheorem}

\subsection{Hypergraph Ramsey Numbers}

Nothing in the question needs the things being coloured to be the edges of a graph, and our motivation for going further is the following result about sequences, which we state without proof.

\begin{boxtheorem}[\Erdos-Szekeres]
    If $n \geq \parenth{r - 1}\parenth{s - 1} + 1$, then any real sequence $a_1, \ldots, a_n$ has a non-decreasing subsequence of length $r$ or a decreasing subsequence of length $s$.
\end{boxtheorem}

To ask the question of hypergraphs we need the hypergraph with every possible hyper-edge.

\begin{boxdefinition}[Complete Uniform Hypergraph]
    Define the \textbf{complete $r$-uniform hypergraph on $n$ vertices}, denoted $K_{(r)}(n)$, to be the $r$-hypergraph on $n$ vertices with all possible hyper-edges.
\end{boxdefinition}

What we look for inside it is the analogue of a monochromatic $K_k$.

% [CORRECTED] was: "a colouring on that hypergraph", and "any red/blue colouring of $K_{(r)}(n)$" in the next box ---
% a colouring of a hypergraph colours its vertices in these notes (the definition in 2.1.2, Colourings of
% Hypergraphs), and read that way $R_{(r)}(k, \ell)$ would be $k + \ell - 1$ whenever $k, \ell \geq r$, whatever
% $r$ is; the proof below colours hyper-edges, and $R_{(2)}(k, k)$ is $R(k)$ only if they are.
% [CORRECTED] was: "The \textbf{size} of a $k$-clique is the number of edges." --- every use of size counts vertices:
% the arguments of $R_{(r)}$ do, and the clique of size $A = R_{(r)}(k - 1, \ell)$ in the proof below is what $R_{(r - 1)}$
% produces, which is $A$ vertices and $\binom{A}{r - 1}$ hyper-edges.
\begin{boxdefinition}[$k$-clique]
    Given a hypergraph and a colouring of its hyper-edges, a \textbf{$k$-clique of some colour} is a set  of $k$ vertices such that all possible hyper-edges on that vertex set are monochromatic, of that colour. The \textbf{size} of a $k$-clique is its number of vertices, $k$.
\end{boxdefinition}

The Ramsey numbers of graphs then generalise directly.

\begin{boxdefinition}[Hypergraph Ramsey Number]
    Given $r, k, \ell \in \N$, we define $R_{(r)}(k, \ell)$ to be the least $n$ such that any red/blue colouring of the hyper-edges of $K_{(r)}(n)$ contains a red $k$-clique or a blue $\ell$-clique.
\end{boxdefinition}

For small $r$ these are familiar objects. $K_{(2)}(n)$ is just $K_n$, so $R_{(2)}(k, k)$ is the Ramsey number $R(k)$ above, and in $K_{(1)}(n)$ each vertex is a hyper-edge by itself. Below are $K_{(1)}(4)$, $K_{(2)}(4)$ and $K_{(3)}(4)$, the last with its four hyper-edges drawn as triangles: three shaded ones, and the outline of the fourth around them.

One easy fact to see is the following.

\begin{boxlemma} \label{Ch2:Lemma:Hypergraph_Ramsey_1}
    $R_{(1)}(k, \ell) = k + \ell - 1$.
\end{boxlemma}
\begin{proof}
    % [FILLED] proof supplied here; the lecture stated the lemma without proving it
    In $K_{(1)}(n)$ every hyper-edge is a single vertex, so colouring the hyper-edges colours the vertices, and a red $k$-clique is just $k$ red vertices. If $n \geq k + \ell - 1$, then fewer than $k$ red vertices leaves at least $\ell$ blue ones. If $n \leq k + \ell - 2$, colouring at most $k - 1$ vertices red and at most $\ell - 1$ blue gives neither.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A Recursive Upper Bound}

We can compute a similar bound to what we know about Ramsey numbers on graphs (\Cref{Ch2:Thm:Ramsey_Recursion}).

% [CORRECTED] was: "For all $r, k, \ell$" --- the right-hand side needs $R_{(r - 1)}$, $R_{(r)}(k - 1, \cdot)$ and $R_{(r)}(\cdot, \ell - 1)$,
% and these definitions give the first only for $r \geq 2$ and the others only for $k, \ell \geq 2$.
\begin{boxtheorem} \label{Ch2:Thm:Hypergraph_Ramsey_Recursion}
    For all $r, k, \ell \geq 2$, we have
    \begin{align*}
        R_{(r)}(k, \ell) \le R_{(r - 1)}\of{R_{(r)}\of{k - 1, \ell}, R_{(r)}\of{k, \ell - 1}} + 1
    \end{align*}
\end{boxtheorem}
\begin{proof}
    Write $A = R_{(r)}(k - 1, \ell)$ and $B = R_{(r)}(k, \ell - 1)$, and take $n = R_{(r - 1)}(A, B) + 1$.

    % [CORRECTED] was: "It has $R_{(n - 1)}\of{A, B}$ neighbours." (the $n - 1$ a slip for $r - 1$), and $H$ with no vertex set named --- when $n < r$, as at
    % $r = 3$ and $k = \ell = 2$, $K_{(r)}(n)$ has no hyper-edges and $v$ no neighbours; what the proof uses is the $n - 1$ other vertices.
    Consider $K_{(r)}(n)$, the complete $r$-uniform hypergraph on $[n]$. Consider any red/blue colouring of its edges. Fix $v \in V\of{K_{(r)}(n)}$. There are $R_{(r - 1)}\of{A, B}$ other vertices.

    % [CORRECTED] was: "removing $v$ from every edge" --- the edges missing $v$ would survive with $r$ vertices, so $H$ would
    % not be $(r - 1)$-uniform, and $R_{(r - 1)}$ could not be applied to it.
    Denote by $H$ the $(r - 1)$-uniform hypergraph on those vertices made by removing $v$ from every edge containing $v$. Every $(r - 1)$-subset $S$ of those vertices is the shadow $\parenth{S \cup \set{v}} \setminus \set{v}$ of exactly one edge, so $H$ is a copy of $K_{(r - 1)}(n - 1)$. Define a ``shadow colouring'' of $H$ by letting the colour of the ``shadow edge'' $f \setminus \set{v}$ be just the colour of $f$.

    By induction/definition of $R_{(r - 1)}$, the shadow contains either a red clique of size $A$ or a blue clique of size $B$.

    \begin{description}
        \item[\underline{Case A: Shadow colouring has a red clique of size $A$.}] Let $W$ be its vertex set, so that $\abs{W} = A = R_{(r)}(k - 1, \ell)$ and every $r$-set $S \cup \set{v}$ with $S$ an $(r - 1)$-subset of $W$ is red. The original colouring of the $r$-subsets of $W$ is a red/blue colouring of a copy of $K_{(r)}(A)$, so $W$ contains either a blue $\ell$-clique, and we are done, or a red $(k - 1)$-clique $U$. In the second case $U \cup \set{v}$ is a red $k$-clique: an $r$-subset of it either lies inside $U$, and is red because $U$ is a red clique, or is $S \cup \set{v}$ for an $(r - 1)$-subset $S$ of $U \ssq W$, and is red because $W$ is a red clique of the shadow.
        \item[\underline{Case B: Shadow colouring has a blue clique of size $B$.}] The same argument with the colours exchanged: its vertex set has $B = R_{(r)}(k, \ell - 1)$ vertices, so it contains either a red $k$-clique, and we are done, or a blue $(\ell - 1)$-clique $U$, and then $U \cup \set{v}$ is a blue $\ell$-clique.
    \end{description}

    Either way, the colouring has a red $k$-clique or a blue $\ell$-clique, so $R_{(r)}(k, \ell) \leq n = R_{(r - 1)}(A, B) + 1$.
\end{proof}

% [FILLED] this paragraph, the corollary and its proof supplied on the author's request (2026-09-30), not from the lecture
At $r = 2$ the bound, together with \Cref{Ch2:Lemma:Hypergraph_Ramsey_1}, reads $R(k, \ell) \leq R(k - 1, \ell) + R(k, \ell - 1)$, which is \Cref{Ch2:Thm:Ramsey_Recursion}. More importantly, the recursion is what shows that these numbers exist at all: the definition asks for a least $n$, and nothing so far has produced even one.

\begin{boxcorollary} \label{Ch2:Cor:Hypergraph_Ramsey_Finite}
    For all $r, k, \ell \in \N$, the Ramsey number $R_{(r)}(k, \ell)$ exists.
\end{boxcorollary}
\begin{proof}
    Induct on $r$. The case $r = 1$ is \Cref{Ch2:Lemma:Hypergraph_Ramsey_1}. For $r \geq 2$, induct on $k + \ell$. If $k = 1$ or $\ell = 1$, then $R_{(r)}(k, \ell) = 1$, since a single vertex has no $r$-subsets and so is vacuously a clique of both colours. Otherwise $k, \ell \geq 2$, so $R_{(r)}(k - 1, \ell)$ and $R_{(r)}(k, \ell - 1)$ exist by the induction on $k + \ell$, $R_{(r - 1)}$ of them exists by the induction on $r$, and the proof of \Cref{Ch2:Thm:Hypergraph_Ramsey_Recursion} exhibits an $n$ with the required property.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A Better Upper Bound}

\Cref{Ch2:Thm:Hypergraph_Ramsey_Recursion} is a recursive bound on hypergraph Ramsey numbers, and it is worth seeing how large the numbers it produces are. Recall that
\begin{align*}
    R_{(r)}\of{k, \ell} \leq R_{(r - 1)}\of{R_{(r)}\of{k-1, \ell}, R_{(r)}\of{k, \ell - 1}} + 1
\end{align*}
Recall also that
\begin{align*}
    R_{(2)}\of{k, \ell}
    \leq \binom{k + \ell - 2}{k - 1}
    \leq 2^{k + \ell}
\end{align*}
We can apply this to show, for $r = 3$, that
\begin{align*}
    R_{(3)}\of{k, \ell}
    & \leq R_{(2)}\of{R_{(3)}\of{k - 1, \ell}, R_{(3)}\of{k, \ell - 1}} + 1 \\
    & \leq 2^{R_{(3)}(k - 1, \ell) + R_{(3)}(k, \ell - 1)} \\
    & \leq \left. 2^{2^{\cdot^{\cdot^{\cdot^{2}}}}} \right\} \text{roughly } k + \ell
\end{align*}
We can apply this to bound $R_{(4)}$ as well:
\begin{align*}
    R_{(4)}\of{k, \ell}
    & \leq R_{(3)}\of{R_{(4)}\of{k - 1, \ell}, R_{(4)}\of{k, \ell - 1}} + 1 \\
    & \leq \left. 2^{2^{\cdot^{\cdot^{\cdot^{2}}}}} \right\} \text{roughly } R_{(4)}\of{k - 1, \ell} + R_{(4)}\of{k, \ell - 1} \\
    & \leq \underbrace{\left. 2^{2^{\cdot^{\cdot^{\cdot^{2}}}}} \right\} \left. 2^{2^{\cdot^{\cdot^{\cdot^{2}}}}} \right\} \cdots \left. 2^{2^{\cdot^{\cdot^{\cdot^{2}}}}} \right\} 2}_{k + \ell \text{ towers}}
\end{align*}
This is a ``super-tower'' of depth $k + \ell$. As you can imagine, doing the same for $R_{(5)}$ gives a ``super-super-tower'', and the upper-bound gets incredibly large incredibly quickly. What this tells us is that we need to improve the upper-bound substantially if we want any hope of estimating hypergraph Ramsey numbers computationally.

We will do two things:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Improve the bound on $R_{(r)}(k, \ell)$
    \item Improve the bound even further when we're dealing with convex problems
\end{enumerate}

Our strategy will be to find an $n$ large enough so that any red/blue colouring of $K_{(r)}(n)$ admits a red $k$-clique or a blue $\ell$-clique. This $n$ will serve as our upper-bound for $R_{(r)}(k, \ell)$. We will do this by constructing a sequence that contains either a $(k - 1)$-clique or an $(\ell - 1)$-clique that can be extended to a $k$-clique or an $\ell$-clique by the addition of another element.

Let's begin by improving the upper-bound on $R_{(3)}(k, \ell)$. We'll do this by constructing a sequence $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_m, x_{m + 1}$ of vertices of our hypergraph such that the colour of $\set{x_a, x_b, x_c}$ depends only on $a$ and $b$ for any $a < b < c$. In other words, the colour of $x_c$ should be a \textit{completion} of the colouring of $x_a$ and $x_b$.\footnote{$a, b, c$ may not be consecutive: in fact, we will often have rather sparse sequences in the hypergraph.}

Pick some $x_1$ and $x_2$. At least half of the remaining vertices complete it in a consistent colour\footnote{ie, all of the remaining vertices complete it \textit{in the same way}/\textit{with the same colour}}. \textit{Throw away the rest.} Pick an $x_3$ from what remains. To pick $x_4$, repeat this process by considering completions of the pair $x_1, x_3$ as well as the pair $x_2, x_3$ (we're already in the $\sim$half consisting of things that consistently complete $x_1, x_2$). Keep going. Indeed, the number of remaining vertices gets very small very quickly. If you start off with (roughly) $n = 2^{\binom{m}{2}}$ (or maybe $n = 2^{\binom{m}{2}} + \text{a small number}$) vertices in your hypergraph, you can choose $x_1, \ldots, x_m$ via this process, and set $x_{m + 1}$ to be what's left. We need to now choose $m$ so that this induces a red $k$-clique or a blue $\ell$-clique, and this gives us our Ramsey upper-bound $n$.

Suppose $m = R_{(2)}\of{k - 1, \ell - 1}$. Then in the colouring induced on pairs in our sequence, we can find a red $(k - 1)$-clique or a blue $(\ell - 1)$-clique. Adding $x_{m + 1}$ then gives us either a red $k$ $(3)$-clique or a blue $\ell$ $(3)$-clique. This gives us an upper-bound
\begin{align*}
    R_{(3)}\of{k, \ell} \leq 2^{\binom{m}{2} + 1} \leq 2^{m^2}
\end{align*}
(the $+1$ takes care of the ``small number'' from before). Since $m \leq 2^{k + \ell}$, we can upper-bound the entire thing by
\begin{align*}
    R_{(3)}\of{k, \ell} \leq 2^{2^{2(k + \ell)}}
\end{align*}

Now let's compute the bound on $R_{(r)}(k, \ell)$.

\begin{boxtheorem}
    For all $r, k, \ell$ sufficiently large, we have the upper-bound
    \begin{align*}
        R_{(r)}\of{k, \ell} \leq 2^{\binom{m}{r - 1} + 1} \leq 2^{m^{r - 1}} = 2^{R_{(r - 1)}\of{k - 1, \ell - 1}^{r - 1}}
    \end{align*}
    where $m = R_{(r - 1)}\of{k - 1, \ell - 1}$.
\end{boxtheorem}
\begin{proof}
    We'll proceed in an identical fashion: Given a red/blue colouring of $K_{(r)}(n)$, build a sequence $x_1, \ldots, x_m, x_{m + 1}$ such that the colour of any $\set{x_{i_1}, \ldots, x_{i_r}}$ (for $i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_r$) is determined by $x_{i_1}, \ldots, x_{i_{r - 1}}$. For $n = 2^{\binom{m}{r - 1}}$, we can make this sequence.

    It remains to choose $m$ so that this sequence has the clique-property. Take $m = R_{(r - 1)}\of{k - 1, \ell - 1}$. Then, similarly to the previous case, in the colouring induced on sets of $r - 1$ elements in our sequence, we can find a red $(k - 1)$-clique or a blue $(\ell - 1)$-clique. Adding $x_{m + 1}$ then gives us either a red $k$ $(r)$-clique or a blue $\ell$ $(r)$-clique. This gives us the upper-bound
    \begin{align*}
        R_{(r)}\of{k, \ell} \leq 2^{\binom{m}{r - 1} + 1} \leq 2^{m^{r - 1}} = 2^{R_{(r - 1)}\of{k - 1, \ell - 1}^{r - 1}}
    \end{align*}
    (the $+1$ again takes care of the ``small number'').
\end{proof}

\subsection{Cups and Caps}

We now move on to a result by \Erdos\ and Szekeres.

\begin{boxdefinition}[Cups and Caps]
    A \textbf{$k$-cap} and a \textbf{$k$-cup} are (respectively) concave and convex arrangements of $k$ points.
\end{boxdefinition}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale = 0.8]
        \drawcup{5}
        \node at (0, -0.7) {a $5$-cup};
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \hspace{2cm}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale = 0.8]
        \drawcap{5}
        \node at (0, -1.7) {a $5$-cap};
    \end{tikzpicture}
\end{figure}

We are interested in how many points it takes to force one of these.

\begin{boxdefinition}[\Erdos--Szekeres Number]
    The \textbf{\Erdos--Szekeres number for $k$ and $\ell$}, denoted $\ES(k, \ell)$, is the smallest $n \in \N$ such that any $n$ points in $\R^2$ in general position (with no two on a vertical line) have a $k$-cap or an $\ell$-cup.
\end{boxdefinition}

We will show, eventually, that
\begin{align*}
    \ES(k, \ell) \le 2\binom{k + \ell - 4}{k - 2} \le 2^{k + \ell}
\end{align*}

% [CORRECTED] was: no hypothesis on $k, \ell$ --- at $k = \ell = 2$ the right-hand side is $\ES(1, 2) + \ES(2, 1) - 1 = 1$ while $\ES(2, 2) = 2$, and the proof uses $q_{k - 2}$ and $p_2$, which need $k, \ell \geq 3$; the corollary below only ever applies the lemma there
\begin{boxlemma}\label{Ch2:Lemma:ES_Recursion}
    For all $k, \ell \geq 3$,
    \begin{align*}
        \ES(k, \ell) \le \ES(k - 1, \ell) + \ES(k, \ell - 1) - 1
    \end{align*}
\end{boxlemma}
\begin{proof}
    We fix an arbitrary set $X \ssq \R^2$ of $\ES(k - 1, \ell) + \ES(k, \ell - 1) - 1$ points in general position, no two on a vertical line, and show that it contains a $k$-cap or an $\ell$-cup.
    
    Deny. Let $L \ssq X$ be the set of points in $X$ that are the last points in some $(k - 1)$-cap, so that $X \setminus L$ has no $(k - 1)$-caps. Thus
    \begin{align*}
        \abs{X \setminus L} < \ES(k - 1, \ell)
    \end{align*}
    Since $\abs{X} = \ES(k - 1, \ell) + \ES(k, \ell - 1) - 1$, we conclude that $\abs{L} \geq \ES(k, \ell - 1)$.

    As $X$ has no $k$-cap, neither does $L$, so $L$ must have an $(\ell - 1)$-cup $p_1, \ldots, p_{\ell - 1}$. Since $p_1 \in L$, it is the last point of some $(k - 1)$-cap $q_1, \ldots, q_{k - 1} = p_1$. One of these two extends, according to how the slopes compare at $p_1$: let $s$ be the slope of $q_{k - 2} p_1$ and $t$ the slope of $p_1 p_2$. These are different, since otherwise $q_{k - 2}$, $p_1$ and $p_2$ would lie on a line.

    \begin{figure}[H] % Note: This was generated by Claude code, though I must admit I had to provide more aesthetic guidance than I anticipated...

    \begin{description}
        \item[\underline{$s > t$.}] The slopes keep decreasing past $p_1$, so $q_1, \ldots, q_{k - 1}, p_2$ is a $k$-cap.
        \item[\underline{$s < t$.}] The slopes are already increasing at $p_1$, so $q_{k - 2}, p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_{\ell - 1}$ is an $\ell$-cup.
    \end{description}
    Either way, we have a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{boxcorollary}
    For all $k, \ell \geq 2$,
    \begin{align*}
        \ES(k, \ell) \leq 2\binom{k + \ell - 4}{k - 2} \leq 2^{k + \ell}
    \end{align*}
\end{boxcorollary}
\begin{proof}
    We prove the first inequality by induction on $k + \ell$. When $k = 2$ or $\ell = 2$, any two points form both a $2$-cap and a $2$-cup, so $\ES(k, \ell) \leq 2 = 2\binom{k + \ell - 4}{k - 2}$.

    Fix some $k, \ell \geq 3$ and assume the result holds for all $k', \ell'$ with $k' + \ell' < k + \ell$. We then get, by \Cref{Ch2:Lemma:ES_Recursion}
    \begin{align*}
        \ES(k, \ell)
        &\leq \ES(k - 1, \ell) + \ES(k, \ell - 1) \\
        &\leq 2\binom{k + \ell - 5}{k - 3} + 2\binom{k + \ell - 5}{k - 2} \\
        &= 2\binom{k + \ell - 4}{k - 2}
    \end{align*}
    with the second inequality given by the induction hypothesis.
    
    For the second inequality, $\binom{k + \ell - 4}{k - 2} \leq 2^{k + \ell - 4}$, since a single binomial coefficient is at most the sum of its row of Pascal's triangle, so $2\binom{k + \ell - 4}{k - 2} \leq 2^{k + \ell - 3} \leq 2^{k + \ell}$.
\end{proof}
