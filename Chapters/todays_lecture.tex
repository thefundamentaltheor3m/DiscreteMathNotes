The arguments that follow all run the same way. We argue by contradiction, and fix a nonzero polynomial $p$ that vanishes on the set we care about, of degree as small as \Cref{Ch7:Lemma:Low_Degree_Vanishing} allows; if the set is small, so is this degree. We then show that $p$ vanishes at many more points than we asked it to, usually by restricting it to a line: there it is a polynomial in one variable of degree at most $\deg(p)$, so if it vanishes at more than $\deg(p)$ points of the line, it vanishes on all of it. Finally, we show that a polynomial of such small degree that vanishes at so many points must be the zero polynomial, which contradicts our choice of $p$. This last step is the delicate one, because vanishing everywhere is not enough on its own: over $\F_q$ we need the degree to be at most $q - 1$ (\Cref{Ch7:Lemma:Vanishing_Everywhere}), and over $\R$ we take $p$ of least degree and differentiate.

The degree bound comes straight out of the dimension count.

\begin{boxlemma} \label{Ch7:Lemma:Low_Degree_Vanishing}
    If $S \ssq \F^n$ is finite, there is a nonzero polynomial of degree at most $n \abs{S}^{\frac{1}{n}}$ that vanishes on $S$.
\end{boxlemma}
\begin{proof}
    Take $D = n \abs{S}^{\frac{1}{n}}$. Then $\frac{D^n}{n!} = \binom{D + n}{n} = \dim_{\F}\of{\poly_D\of{\F^n}}$ is greater than $\abs{S}$, so by rank-nullity, the evaluation map has a nontrivial kernel.
\end{proof}

Over $\R$, we can also differentiate. Given $p \in \R\!\brac{x, y, z}$, consider the gradient
\begin{align*}
    \nabla p = \parenth{\frac{\partial}{\partial x} p, \frac{\partial}{\partial y} p, \frac{\partial}{\partial z} p}
\end{align*}
At any point $b = \parenth{b_0, b_1, b_2}$, and for any unit vector $a$, the directional derivative of $p$ at $b$ in the direction of $a$ is given by $\cycl{\nabla p (b), a}$.

    Consider a nonzero polynomial $p$ over $\R$ in $3$ variables of the least degree $D \leq J^{\frac{1}{3}}$ that vanishes on the joints. Such a polynomial exists by \Cref{Ch7:Lemma:Low_Degree_Vanishing}.

    We've assumed every line in our collection participates in $>3 J^{\frac{1}{3}}$. Then for any line $\ell$ of the form $at + b$ with $a, b \in \R^3$, $p(at + b)$ is a single variable polynomial of degree $D \leq 3J^{\frac{1}{3}}$, vanishing at $> D$ points. So $p$ is zero on $L$. In other words, $p$ is zero on all lines in our collection.

    Given a joint $\sigma$ there are three directions $a_1, a_2, a_3$ of lines through $\nabla p (\sigma)$. There may well be more than three lines of our collection through $\sigma$, but by definition three of them are not coplanar, and those are the three we take. These three directions form a basis.

    The directional derivatives of $p$ at $\sigma$ in all these directions $a_1, a_2, a_3$ must be zero. Indeed, $p$ vanishes on the line $\setst{a_i t + \sigma}{t \in \R}$, so $t \mapsto p\of{a_i t + \sigma}$ is the zero polynomial, and its derivative at $t = 0$, which by the chain rule is $\cycl{\nabla p (\sigma), a_i}$, is zero. So $\nabla p (\sigma)$ must itself be zero, because its inner product with all elements of a basis is zero. In particular, all three partial derivatives also vanish at every joint. But this necessitates that either one of these is a nonzero polynomial of smaller degree than $p$ or $p$ is the zero polynomial. Either way, we have a contradiction.
\end{proof}

Recall that a polynomial evaluating to zero on all inputs does not make it the zero polynomial.

\begin{boxcexample}
    The polynomial $x(x - 1) \in \F_2[x]$ evaluates to zero at all points in $\F_2$ but is not equal to the zero polynomial.
\end{boxcexample}

But it turns out that under some conditions we can make this fact true.

\begin{boxlemma} \label{Ch7:Lemma:Vanishing_Everywhere}
    If $p \in \poly_{q - 1}\of{\F_q^{n-1}}$ and $p(x) = 0$ for all $x \in \F_q^{n - 1}$, then $p$ is the zero polynomial.
\end{boxlemma}
\begin{proof}
    Argue by induction on $n$. When $n = 1$, the result follows by polynomial division and applying the fact that each single-variable nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ has at most $d$ roots.

    For the inductive step, consider a polynomial $p(x) \in \poly_{q - 1}\of{\F_q^n}$. Write
    \begin{align*}
        p\of{x_1, \ldots, x_n} = \sum_{j=0}^{q-1} p_j\of{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_{n - 1}} x_n^j
    \end{align*}
    for some polynomials $p_j \in \F_{q}\brac{x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}}$. Each $p_j$ is zero by the induction hypothesis and each $x_n^j$ is also zero by the single-variable case.
\end{proof}

\begin{boxdefinition}[Nikodym Set]
    A set $N \ssq \F_q^n$ is \textbf{Nikodym} if $\forall x \in \F_q^n$, $\exists L(x)$ a line such that all points of $L(x)$ except for $x$ itself are in $N$.
\end{boxdefinition}

In $\R^2$ it means the unit square has a subset $N$ of measure zero such that every point $x$ of the square outside $N$ lies on a line whose other points in the square all lie in $N$. That such a subset exists is a theorem of Nikodym, from 1927, so in $\R^2$ a set of this kind can be negligible. Over $\F_q$ this is far from the case.\footnote{The method is Dvir's, from his proof of the finite field Kakeya conjecture: Z.~Dvir, \textit{On the size of Kakeya sets in finite fields}, J.~Amer.~Math.~Soc.~22 (2009), 1093--1097, posted to the arXiv in March 2008. \begin{boxdefinition}[Kakeya Set]
    A set $K \ssq \F_q^n$ is \textbf{Kakeya} if $\forall a \in \F_q^n \setminus \set{0}$, $\exists b \in \F_q$ such that
    \begin{align*}
        \setst{at + b}{t \in \F_q} \ssq K
    \end{align*}
\end{boxdefinition}
