\section{Erdos Unit Distance Problem}

The \Erdos\ Unit Distance Problem asks the following question:
\begin{center}
    \textbf{How many unit distances can there be amongst $n$ distinct points in $\R^2$?}
\end{center}

Some naïve constructions include making each point adjacent to four or six others by way of square/triangular tiling. % [CLAUDE] illustrate this

Another construction gives us even better numbers.

Take a square. Each vertex is connected to $2$ others. Translate it by one unit and connect it. Then each vertex is connected to $3$ others. Translate it by another unit and connect it again. Then each vertex is connected to $4$ others. Keep going. In this construction, you get $n \log(n)$ as your lower-bound. % [CLAUDE] make this more precise. \Erdos\ gave something even better: draw a grid with the points some distance $\frac{1}{R}$ apart, where $R$ has the property that $R^2 = a^2 + b^2$ in many ways. The point is that this looks like the following: % [CLAUDE] draw a grid with a circle of radius R units overlaid on it

We can then do some very nice algebraic number theory involving $\Z[i]$ to get
\begin{align*}
    n^{1 + \frac{c}{\log\of{\log(n)}}}
\end{align*}

\Erdos\ conjectured that the maximum you could get to is $n^{1 + o(1)}$. OpenAI disproved this by finding an $n^{1 + \delta}$ construction for some $\delta > 0$.

We will not get into the lower-bounds here, but we will show an upper-bound: $n^{\frac{4}{3}}$.

The reasons graphs fail to be planar is that too many of their edges cross.

(Connected) plane graphs satisfy Euler's Formula: $v - e + f = 2$. We can prove this using induction on $e$ and some handwaving (one should say ``visual intuition''), but to actually do this rigorously takes a lot of machinery (eg. the Jordan Curve Theorem).

\begin{boxtheorem}[Euler's Formula]
    For a connected plane graph with $v$ vertices, $e$ edges and $f$ faces,
    \begin{align*}
        v - e + f = 2
    \end{align*}
\end{boxtheorem}

Notice that in any plane graph, we \textit{must} have
\begin{itemize}
    \item Every face is incident with $\geq 3$ edges
    \item Every edge is incident with $\leq 2$ faces
\end{itemize}

This gives us an important inequality.

\begin{boxproposition}
    For any connected simple plane graph with $v$ vertices, $e$ edges and $f$ faces, if $e \geq 3$, then
    \begin{align*}
        e \leq 3v - 6
    \end{align*}
\end{boxproposition}
\begin{proof}
    Count edge-face incidences. By above,
    \begin{align*}
        3f \leq \sum_{F \text{ a face}} \#\!\set{\text{edges $F$ is incident with}} = \sum_{E \text{ an edge}} \#\!\set{\text{faces $E$ is incident with}} \leq 2e
    \end{align*}
    Thus, we get the equivalent inequalities
    \begin{align*}
        3f \leq 2e \qquad \iff  \qquad f \leq \frac{2e}{3}
    \end{align*}
    Combining with Euler's formula, we get
    \begin{align*}
        2
        = v - e + f
        \leq v - e + \frac{2e}{3}
        = v - \frac{e}{3}
    \end{align*}
    Rearranging gives us the desired inequality.
\end{proof}

\begin{boxdefinition}[Crossing Number] % [CLAUDE] make a macro for crossing number
    For every finite simple graph $G$, define the \textbf{crossing number}, denoted $cr(G)$, to be the minimum number of crossings between edges, taken over all drawings of $G$ in the plane. Ie, define
    \begin{align*}
        cr(G) = \min_{\text{drawings of $G$ in the plane}} \#\!\set{\text{crossings between edges}}
    \end{align*}
\end{boxdefinition}
