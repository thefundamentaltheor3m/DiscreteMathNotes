\begin{boxlemma} % [CLAUDE] move this proof to the place where the lemma is stated in the notes when you run /integrate. replace my earlier statement with this one.
    In any collection of $L$ lines, some line participates in $\leq 3J^{\frac{1}{3}}$ joints.
\end{boxlemma}
\begin{proof}
    Suppose not. Ie, suppose we have a collection of $L$ lines in which every line participates in $> 3 J^{\frac{1}{3}}$ joints.
    
    Consider a nonzero polynomial $p$ over $\R$ in $3$ variables of the least degree $D \leq J^{\frac{1}{3}}$ that vanishes on the joints. Such a polynomial exists for the following reason: on any set $S$ with $D = n \abs{S}^{\frac{1}{n}}$, $\frac{D^n}{n!} = \binom{D + n}{n} = \dim_{\F}\of{\poly_D\of{\F^n}}$ is greater than $\abs{S}$, so by rank-nullity, the evaluation map has a nontrivial kernel.

    We've assumed every line in our collection participates in $>3 J^{\frac{1}{3}}$. Then for any line $\ell$ of the form $at + b$ with $a, b \in \R^3$, $p(at + b)$ is a single variable polynomial of degree $D \leq 3J^{\frac{1}{3}}$, vanishing at $> D$ points. So $p$ is zero on $L$. In other words, $p$ is zero on all lines in our collection.

    Consider the gradient
    \begin{align*}
        \nabla p = \parenth{\frac{\partial}{\partial x} p, \frac{\partial}{\partial y} p, \frac{\partial}{\partial z} p}
    \end{align*}
    At any point $b = \parenth{b_0, b_1, b_2}$, and for any unit vector $a$, the directional derivative of $p$ at $b$ in the direction of $a$ is given by $\cycl{\nabla p (b), a}$.

    Given a joint $\sigma$ there are three directions $a_1, a_2, a_3$ of lines through $\nabla p (\sigma)$. These three directions form a basis.
    
    % [CLAUDE] illustrate: draw a joint with three points

    % [FILLED] the reason the directional derivatives vanish was a \todo{why?}
    The directional derivatives of $p$ at $\sigma$ in all these directions $a_1, a_2, a_3$ must be zero. Indeed, $p$ vanishes on the line $\setst{a_i t + \sigma}{t \in \R}$, so $t \mapsto p\of{a_i t + \sigma}$ is the zero polynomial, and its derivative at $t = 0$, which by the chain rule is $\cycl{\nabla p (\sigma), a_i}$, is zero. So $\nabla p (\sigma)$ must itself be zero, because its inner product with all elements of a basis is zero. In particular, all three partial derivatives also vanish at every joint. But this necessitates that either one of these is a nonzero polynomial of smaller degree than $p$ or $p$ is the zero polynomial. Either way, we have a contradiction.
\end{proof}

Recall that a polynomial evaluating to zero on all inputs does not make it the zero polynomial.

\begin{boxcexample}
    The polynomial $x(x - 1) \in \F_2[x]$ evaluates to zero at all points in $\F_2$ but is not equal to the zero polynomial.
\end{boxcexample}

But it turns out that under some conditions we can make this fact true.

\begin{boxlemma}
    If $p \in \poly_{q - 1}\of{\F_q^{n-1}}$ and $p(x) = 0$ for all $x \in \F_q^{n - 1}$, then $p$ is the zero polynomial.
\end{boxlemma}
\begin{proof}
    Argue by induction on $n$. When $n = 1$, the result follows by polynomial division and applying the fact that each single-variable nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ has at most $d$ roots.

    For the inductive step, consider a polynomial $p(x) \in \poly_{q - 1}\of{\F_q^n}$. Write
    \begin{align*}
        p\of{x_1, \ldots, x_n} = \sum_{j=0}^{q-1} p_j\of{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_{n - 1}} x_n^j
    \end{align*}
    for some polynomials $p_j \in \F_{q}\brac{x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}}$. Each $p_j$ is zero by the induction hypothesis and each $x_n^j$ is also zero by the single-variable case.
\end{proof}

\begin{boxdefinition}[Nikodym Set]
    A set $N \ssq \F_q^n$ is \textbf{Nikodym} if $\forall x \in \F_q^n$, $\exists L(x)$ a line such that all points of $L(x)$ except for $x$ itself are in $N$.
\end{boxdefinition}

In $\R^2$ it means the unit square has a subset $N$ of measure zero such that every point $x$ of the square outside $N$ lies on a line whose other points in the square all lie in $N$. \begin{boxdefinition}[Kakeya Set]
    A set $K \ssq \F_q^n$ is \textbf{Kakeya} if $\forall a \in \F_q^n \setminus \set{0}$, $\exists b \in \F_q$ such that
    \begin{align*}
        \setst{at + b}{t \in \F_q} \ssq K
    \end{align*}
\end{boxdefinition}
