Let's now ask ourselves a question that, though seemingly similar to the unit distance problem, is actually quite different:
\begin{center}
    How many points in $\R^d$ can all be at pairwise unit distance?
\end{center}
In the unit distance problem, we \textit{fixed points} and \textit{counted unit distances}. Here, we haven't fixed any points.

It turns out the answer is $d + 1$, and is given by the regular simplex.

Let's study the properties of hypothetical points that would satisfy this condition to glean information that will help us figure out what they might be.

\subsection{Eventown and Oddtown}

Wes reckons this place is close to Allentown. Gotta explore Eastern Pennsylvania some more.

The warm and friendly people of Eventown, Pennsylvania, love forming clubs. They do so in the following way.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item All pairs of clubs have an \underline{even} number of people in common.
    \item All clubs have an \underline{even} number of people.
\end{enumerate}
We can show that if there are $n$ people in Eventown, then there can be at most $2^{\frac{n}{2}}$ clubs.

Not too far from Eventown, Pennsylvania, is the town of Oddtown, Pennsylvania. A rather \textit{odd} place (\textit{even} more so than Eventown), Oddtown also has a lot of clubs, but in Oddtown,
\begin{enumerate}
    \item All pairs of clubs have an \underline{even} number of people in common.
    \item All clubs have an \underline{odd} number of people.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Fisher's Inequality}

% [CORRECTED] was: "Fix a collection $\A \ssq \Powset{[n]}$. If ..., then $\abs{\A} = n$." --- eg, $\set{\set{1, 2}, \set{1, 3}}$ has $k = 1$ and $2 < 3$ sets; and at $k = 0$, $\set{\emptyset, \set{1}, \ldots, \set{n}}$ has $n + 1$ (the proof's last step uses $k \neq 0$)
\begin{boxtheorem}[Fisher's Inequality]
    Fix a collection $\A \ssq \Powset{[n]}$ and $k \geq 1$. If $\abs{A \cap B} = k$ for all distinct $A, B \in \A$, then $\abs{\A} \leq n$.
\end{boxtheorem}
\begin{proof}
    Let $v_A$ denote the incidence vector for $A \in \A$ (over $\R$). This is the vector in $\R^n$ whose $i$th coordinate is $1$ if $i \in A$ and $0$ otherwise, so that $\cycl{v_A, v_B} = \abs{A \cap B}$.
    We want to show that $\set{v_A}_{A \in \A}$ is linearly independent.

    Suppose $0 = \sum_{A \in \A} \lambda_A v_A$. Then
    \begin{align*}
        0
        &= \cycl{\sum_{A \in \A} \lambda_A v_A , \sum_{B \in \A} \lambda_B v_B} \\
        &= \sum_{A \in \A } \lambda_A^2 \abs{A} + \sum_{A \neq B} k \lambda_A \lambda_B \\
        &= k\parenth{\sum_{A \in \A} \lambda_A}^2 + \sum_{A \in \A}\parenth{\abs{A} - k} \lambda_A^2
    \end{align*}
    If $\A$ has at most one set, there is nothing to prove, so suppose it has at least two. Then every $A \in \A$ has $\abs{A} \geq k$, since it meets the other sets in $k$ elements, and there is at most one $A_0 \in \A$ with $\abs{A_0} = k$, since two such sets would both be equal to their intersection. So both terms on the right are nonnegative, and as they add up to $0$, both are $0$. The second gives $\lambda_A = 0$ for every $A \neq A_0$, and then the first gives $\lambda_{A_0} = \sum_{A \in \A} \lambda_A = 0$ also. So the $v_A$ are linearly independent vectors in $\R^n$, and there are at most $n$ of them.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Points and Lines}

Say we have $n$ points that are not all on a line. This means that they determine $\geq n$ lines.

Let $P$ be a set of points determining a set $L$ of lines. For a point $p \in P$, let $A_p$ be the set of lines in $L$ that are incident with $p$. For points $p \neq q$, would $\abs{A_p \cap A_q} = 1$ imply that $\abs{P} \leq \abs{L}$?

If not all points are on one line, then for all $p \neq q$, $A_p \neq A_q$. So there is a point $r$ that does not lie on the line $\bar{pq}$. Thus, the line $\bar{pr} \in A_p \setminus A_q$. Then $\bar{qr} \in A_q \setminus A_p$.

So the $A_p$, for $p \in P$, are $\abs{P}$ distinct subsets of $L$, and any two of them, $A_p$ and $A_q$, meet in exactly one line, namely $\bar{pq}$. Fisher's Inequality, with $L$ in place of $[n]$ and $k = 1$, now gives $\abs{P} \leq \abs{L}$. So the answer to our question is yes: $n$ points, not all on a line, determine at least $n$ lines.

\subsection{A Lower Bound on Ramsey Numbers}

Yet another application.

Recall our Ramsey number bound $R(k) = R(k, k) \geq (k - 1)^2 + 1$, which comes from $k - 1$ disjoint copies of $K_{k - 1}$: they have no clique and no independent set of size $k$. Fisher's Inequality gives a much better construction for large $k$.

Define a graph $G$ with vertex set $T = \binom{[k]}{3}$. In other words, the vertex set is the set of all subsets of $[k]$ of size $3$.

For $A, B \in T$, say $A \sim B$ if $\abs{A \cap B} = 1$. Using this relation, define the edge set of the graph $G$.

Fisher's Inequality tells us that $\omega(G) \leq k$, where $\omega(G)$ is the clique number (see \Cref{Ch2:Def:Clique}): a clique is a collection of $3$-subsets of $[k]$ any two of which meet in exactly one element.

In other words, we're living in oddtown!" --- $\abs{I} \leq k$ is not part of being independent, it is what living in Oddtown gives; and the condition is for distinct $A, B$, since $\abs{A \cap A} = 3$ (review rounds one and two)
An independent set in this context would be a set $I$ where $\abs{A \cap B} \in \set{0, 2}$ for all distinct $A, B \in I$. In other words, we're living in oddtown! So $\abs{I} \leq k$. The sets in $I$ are the clubs of an Oddtown with $k$ people: each has $3$ people, an odd number, and any two share $0$ or $2$ of them, an even number.

So $G$ has $\binom{k}{3}$ vertices, and neither a clique nor an independent set of more than $k$ of them. We can then show $R(k + 1) > \binom{k}{3}$.
